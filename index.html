How to Protect Your Data: A Practical Guide

Most data loss and data theft doesn't happen through some sophisticated, unstoppable attack. It happens through a handful of predictable gaps no backup, a reused password, an unpatched device, a phishing email that got clicked without a second thought. The good news is that closing most of these gaps doesn't require deep technical knowledge. It requires consistent habits.

Here's what actually matters, in order of how much it protects you relative to how much effort it takes.

Back up your data - and actually test the backup

This is the single most important thing on this list, and the one most people skip until after they've lost something. A backup means that even if ransomware locks your files, your laptop dies, or you accidentally delete something important, you have a separate copy that isn't affected. The 3-2-1 rule is a reasonable standard to aim for: 3 copies of your data, on 2 different types of storage, with 1 copy kept somewhere physically separate (cloud storage, or an external drive kept in a different location). Backing up isn't enough on its own a backup you've never tried to restore from is a backup you don't actually know works. Periodically check that you can actually open and use a backed-up file, not just that the backup process completed without an error.

Use unique passwords for every account

Reusing a password across multiple accounts means that if one service you use gets breached, an attacker can try that same password on your email, banking, and social media accounts, too. This is called credential stuffing, and it's one of the most common ways accounts get compromised without any direct attack on the account itself. A password manager solves this practically - it generates and stores a unique, strong password for every account, so you only need to remember one master password. This is a much more realistic solution than trying to memorize dozens of unique passwords yourself.

Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA)

2FA requires a second piece of verification beyond your password - usually a code from an app, a text message, or a physical security key - before granting access. Even if a password is stolen or guessed, 2FA generally stops an attacker from getting in without also having access to your second factor. Prioritize enabling this on your email account first. Email is often the recovery method for every other account you own, which makes it the single highest-value account to protect properly.

Keep your software and devices updated

Many attacks exploit known vulnerabilities that have already been patched the issue isn't that a fix doesn't exist, it's that the update hasn't been installed yet. This applies to operating systems, browsers, apps, and router firmware, which is a commonly overlooked one. Turning on automatic updates where available removes the need to remember to do this manually.

Encrypt sensitive data and devices

Most modern operating systems include built-in encryption (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on Mac) that protects the data on your device if it's lost or stolen - without encryption, someone with physical access to your device can often extract data directly, even without your password. Turning this on is usually a one-time setting change, not an ongoing task. For anything especially sensitive shared over email or cloud storage, consider whether it needs to be encrypted specifically for that transfer, rather than relying only on whatever protection the platform itself provides by default.

Be deliberate about what you share and where

Data exposure isn't always the result of a breach sometimes it's simply oversharing. Review privacy settings on social media periodically, be cautious about what personal details you post publicly (birthdates, addresses, information commonly used in security questions), and think about who can actually see what you're sharing, not just who you intended to share it with.

Recognize phishing before it becomes a data problem

A significant share of data theft starts with a phishing message that leads to stolen credentials or installed malware, not a direct technical attack. Being cautious with unexpected links and attachments, verifying requests for sensitive information through a separate channel, and not acting purely out of urgency addresses this risk directly, without needing any special tools.

Limit what you store and where

Not every piece of data needs to exist in multiple places. Old accounts you no longer use, files stored in outdated cloud services, and unnecessary copies of sensitive documents all represent data that could be exposed even though you're not actively using it. Periodically reviewing and deleting what you no longer need reduces how much is actually at risk if something does go wrong.

Why none of this requires being a security expert

Everything above is a habit, not a specialized skill. The gap between someone who protects their data well and someone who doesn't usually isn't technical knowledge - it's consistency. A password manager, backups, and 2FA cover the majority of realistic risk for most people, and none of the three require understanding how an attack technically works, only committing to setting them up and keeping them on.

The short version

Protecting your data comes down to a small number of habits done consistently: back up your data and actually test the backup, use unique passwords through a password manager, turn on two-factor authentication, keep software updated, encrypt your devices, and stay alert to phishing. None of it is complicated on its own the difficulty is usually just getting around to setting it up, not understanding how.

What Is Ethical Hacking?

Ethical hacking is the practice of deliberately probing computer systems, networks, or applications for security weaknesses - with permission, and with the goal of fixing what's found rather than exploiting it. The techniques involved often look identical to what a malicious attacker would do. The difference is authorization, intent, and what happens with the results afterward.

That distinction matters more than people expect. The exact same skill - finding a way into a system that wasn't supposed to be accessible - is either a crime or a job, depending entirely on whether the person doing it had permission to be there.

Why organizations pay people to hack them

It sounds counterintuitive at first: a company hiring someone to break into its own systems. But the logic is simple. If a vulnerability exists in a piece of software or a network configuration, someone is going to find it eventually. The only question is whether that someone reports it responsibly or uses it to steal data, deploy ransomware, or cause damage. Ethical hackers are hired to find those weaknesses first, document exactly how they got in, and hand that information to the organization so it can be fixed before a real attacker stumbles onto the same gap. This is usually called penetration testing, or sometimes a "pen test," and it's one of the most common paid roles under the ethical hacking umbrella.

The different flavors of ethical hacking

Not all ethical hacking looks the same. A few common forms:

  • Penetration testing: a scoped, time-limited engagement where a tester attacks a specific system or network with the client's permission, then delivers a report of what they found.
  • Bug bounty hunting: companies like Google, Meta, and many others run public programs that pay independent researchers for responsibly reporting vulnerabilities they find in production systems, outside of any formal contract.
  • Red teaming: a broader, often longer engagement that simulates a real adversary's full attack chain, testing not just technical systems but also how an organization's people and processes respond to an active threat.
  • Vulnerability assessment: less about actively breaking in and more about systematically scanning and cataloging weaknesses across a system, often as an earlier or lighter-weight step before a full penetration test.

What separates "ethical" from "illegal"

Three things, generally:

  • Authorization: A written agreement a scope document, a bug bounty program's rules, a signed contract that explicitly permits testing specific systems in specific ways. Testing anything outside that scope, even with good intentions, isn't ethical hacking; it's unauthorized access.
  • Disclosure: Findings go to the organization that owns the system, not to a forum, a buyer, or the public, before the issue is fixed. Ethical hackers who find vulnerabilities in bug bounty programs typically follow a responsible disclosure timeline, giving the company a chance to patch the issue before any public details are shared.
  • Non-destructive intent: The goal is to demonstrate that a weakness exists and how it could be exploited, not to cause the damage a real attacker would. A penetration tester who finds a way to access a database stops at proving access is possible - they don't exfiltrate or destroy real customer data along the way.

Do you need to be a "hacker" to start learning this?

Not in the sense most people picture. Ethical hacking as a field draws on the same foundation as the rest of cyber security networking, operating systems, understanding how applications and protocols actually work applied from an attacker's perspective instead of a defender's. Most people who move into this field start by learning the fundamentals, then practice on legally sanctioned platforms built specifically for this purpose, where breaking things is the entire point and no permission or contract is needed beyond signing up.

The short version

Ethical hacking is authorized, disclosed, non-destructive security testing - using an attacker's mindset and techniques to find weaknesses before someone without permission does. It's a legitimate, in-demand career path, but the "ethical" part isn't optional flavor text. It's the entire thing that separates the job from the crime.

What Is Penetration Testing?

Penetration testing, often shortened to "pen testing," is an authorized, simulated attack against a computer system, network, or application, carried out to find security weaknesses before someone without permission does. It's one of the most well-known practical applications of ethical hacking - where ethical hacking is the broader concept, penetration testing is a specific, structured engagement with a defined scope, timeline, and deliverable.

The word "simulated" matters here. A penetration tester uses many of the same techniques a real attacker would, but the goal is to demonstrate that a weakness exists and explain how it could be exploited, not to cause the damage a genuine attacker would.

Why organizations pay for this

Most organizations have some idea of their security posture on paper - firewall rules, access policies, patch schedules. A penetration test checks whether that theoretical security actually holds up against someone actively trying to break it. It's the difference between assuming a lock works and having someone qualified actually try to pick it. Many industries also require regular penetration testing for compliance reasons standards like PCI DSS (for organizations handling payment card data) explicitly mandate it, which means a portion of the demand for this work comes from regulatory requirements, not just general caution.

How a typical penetration test is structured

  • Scoping and authorization: Before any testing begins, the client and tester agree on exactly what's in scope which systems, which techniques are allowed, and which are off-limits and this is documented in a signed agreement. Testing anything outside this scope isn't penetration testing; it's unauthorized access, regardless of intent.
  • Reconnaissance: The tester gathers information about the target - technologies in use, exposed services, publicly available information that could inform an attack - much of it using the same methods a real attacker would start with.
  • Scanning and vulnerability identification: Tools and manual techniques are used to identify potential weaknesses: outdated software, misconfigurations, weak authentication, exposed services that shouldn't be reachable.
  • Exploitation: The tester attempts to actually exploit identified weaknesses to demonstrate real impact, not just theoretical risk - for example, actually gaining access to a system rather than just noting that a vulnerability exists in principle.
  • Post-exploitation and reporting: If access is gained, a tester typically documents how far that access could realistically extend (this is sometimes discussed in terms of "lateral movement" - how far an attacker could move through connected systems once inside). All of this gets written into a report explaining what was found, how it was achieved, and how severe the risk is.
  • Remediation and retesting: The organization fixes the identified issues, and a follow-up test sometimes confirms the fixes actually worked.

Different types of penetration tests

  • Black box testing: the tester starts with no internal knowledge of the target system, similar to how an external attacker would approach it.
  • White box testing: the tester has full access to internal documentation, source code, or architecture diagrams beforehand, allowing for a deeper and more thorough assessment in less time.
  • Gray box testing: a middle ground, where the tester has some internal knowledge (like a low-level user account) but not full access, simulating what an attacker with partial access like a compromised employee account - might be able to do.

Tests are also commonly categorized by target: network penetration testing, web application penetration testing, wireless penetration testing, and social engineering-focused testing, among others.

What penetration testing is not

It's not the same as a vulnerability scan, which is largely automated and identifies potential weaknesses without confirming they're actually exploitable. A vulnerability scan tells you what might be a problem; a penetration test tells you whether it actually is one, and how bad it would be if it were exploited. It's also not a one-time fix. A penetration test is a snapshot of security at a specific point in time. New vulnerabilities get discovered, systems change, and new software gets deployed - which is why organizations that take this seriously run these tests periodically, not just once.

Getting into this field

Penetration testing is a common specialization within cyber security, but it typically requires solid fundamentals first - networking, operating systems, and a real understanding of how the systems being tested actually work - before the offensive techniques make sense. Most people practice on legally sanctioned platforms built specifically for this purpose before pursuing paid work or certifications in the area, since testing systems without authorization, even out of curiosity, crosses from ethical hacking into a criminal offense.

The short version

Penetration testing is an authorized, structured simulation of a real attack, used to find and prove out security weaknesses before someone without permission finds them first. It's more rigorous and more targeted than a general vulnerability scan, bound by a strict scope agreement, and treated as a recurring practice rather than something an organization does once and considers finished.

What Is a Bug Bounty?

A bug bounty is a program where an organization invites independent security researchers to find and report vulnerabilities in its systems, in exchange for a reward - usually money, though sometimes recognition or other incentives. Instead of hiring a fixed team to test a system once, a bug bounty program stays open on an ongoing basis, with anyone who qualifies free to look for issues and get paid if they find something real.

Companies like Google, Meta, Microsoft, and many others run public bug bounty programs, and there are also platforms - HackerOne and Bugcrowd being the two best known - that host and manage bounty programs on behalf of organizations that don't want to run one entirely in-house.

How it's different from penetration testing

Penetration testing and bug bounty hunting both involve authorized security testing, but the structure is different. A penetration test is a scoped, time-limited engagement a company hires a specific person or team, defines exactly what's being tested, and pays regardless of what's found. A bug bounty program is open-ended and crowd-sourced: any qualifying researcher can participate, there's no fixed timeline, and payment only happens if a valid, previously unreported vulnerability is actually found.

This means bug bounty hunting is inherently less predictable as income - you might spend weeks looking at a target and find nothing payable, or you might find something significant relatively quickly. Penetration testing, by contrast, is paid work regardless of the outcome, since the client is paying for the tester's time and expertise, not for a guaranteed result.

How a typical bug bounty program works

  • Scope definition: Every program publishes exactly what's in scope - which domains, apps, or systems can be tested - and explicitly what's out of bounds. Testing anything outside the published scope isn't protected by the program's rules, even if the intent was good.
  • Researching and testing: Researchers use techniques similar to penetration testing and ethical hacking generally reconnaissance, vulnerability identification, and attempts to demonstrate real impact but working independently, without a client relationship or a fixed schedule.
  • Responsible disclosure: When a vulnerability is found, it's reported privately to the organization through the program's official channel, not posted publicly or sold. Programs typically specify a disclosure timeline, giving the organization a set period to fix the issue before any public details can be shared, if they can be shared at all.
  • Triage and validation: The organization or platform reviews the report to confirm it's valid, not a duplicate of something already reported, and within scope.
  • Reward: If the report is validated, the researcher is paid according to a reward structure tied to the severity of the vulnerability a minor issue might pay a small amount, while a critical, high-impact vulnerability in a major platform can pay significantly more.

Why companies run these instead of relying only on internal testing

Internal security teams and hired penetration testers are limited in number and time. A bug bounty program effectively adds an unlimited, globally distributed pool of researchers looking at a system from different angles and skill sets, on an ongoing basis rather than during a single scheduled test window. For large, complex systems, this breadth can surface issues that a smaller, time-boxed engagement might miss.

What it takes to get started

Bug bounty hunting generally isn't a beginner's first step into ethical hacking it rewards people who already have solid fundamentals and hands-on practice, since payment depends entirely on finding something real, not on effort or time spent. Most people build skill on structured practice platforms first, then move toward bug bounty programs once they have a reasonable grasp of common vulnerability types and how to find them methodically rather than by chance. It's also worth knowing that competition is real popular programs get looked at by large numbers of researchers, and many of the "easy" or obvious vulnerabilities in well-known targets have often already been found and fixed.

What it is not

Finding a vulnerability in a system with no bug bounty program, and no explicit permission to test it, is not protected activity - it's unauthorized access, regardless of good intentions or a plan to report it responsibly. Bug bounty hunting only exists within the boundaries of a program's published scope and rules; acting outside that scope removes the legal protection the program provides.

The short version

A bug bounty is a reward-based, ongoing invitation for independent researchers to find and responsibly report vulnerabilities within a defined scope, paid out based on what's actually found rather than time spent. It's a legitimate and increasingly common path in the ethical hacking field, but one that generally rewards people who've already built solid fundamentals and practical experience elsewhere first.

What Is Network Security?

Network security is the set of practices and technologies used to protect the infrastructure that data travels through - routers, switches, firewalls, wireless access points, and the connections between them from unauthorized access, misuse, or disruption. If cyber security is the broad umbrella, network security is one of its oldest and most foundational branches, because almost every other kind of attack has to travel over a network to reach its target in the first place.

Why the network layer matters so much

Attackers rarely have physical access to the systems they're targeting. Instead, they reach a target device by moving across a network - the internet, a company's internal network, or a public Wi-Fi connection. That means the network itself is often the first line of defense. If it's poorly secured, an attacker doesn't need to break into the actual application or database directly; they just need to find a weak point in how traffic moves between systems. This is why network security tends to come up early in any cyber security education. Understanding how traffic flows, where it can be intercepted, and how to control who's allowed to send or receive it underpins almost everything else in the field.

The core building blocks

  • Firewalls: A firewall sits between networks - often between a private network and the internet - and filters traffic based on rules: which ports are allowed, which IP addresses are permitted, which types of connections are blocked outright. Modern firewalls go further, inspecting the actual content of traffic rather than just its headers.
  • Intrusion detection and prevention systems (IDS/IPS): These monitor network traffic for patterns that match known attacks or suspicious behavior. An IDS flags what it finds; an IPS can actively block it in real time.
  • VPNs (Virtual Private Networks): A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel between two points on a network, so traffic passing through it can't be easily read even if it's intercepted along the way. Businesses commonly use VPNs to let remote employees securely connect to internal systems.
  • Network segmentation: Rather than treating an entire network as one flat space, segmentation divides it into smaller zones with controlled access between them. If an attacker compromises one segment, segmentation limits how far they can move without being stopped or noticed.
  • Access control: Deciding exactly who and what is allowed to connect to a network, and under what conditions often enforced through authentication systems, network access control (NAC) tools, and policies about which devices are permitted to join.

Common threats network security defends against

  • Man-in-the-middle attacks, where an attacker secretly intercepts communication between two parties, sometimes altering it without either side noticing.
  • Denial-of-service (DoS) and distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, which flood a network or server with traffic until it can't respond to legitimate requests.
  • Unauthorized access, where an attacker finds a way onto a network they shouldn't have access to through a weak Wi-Fi password, an unpatched router, or a misconfigured remote access setup.
  • Packet sniffing, where an attacker captures unencrypted traffic traveling across a network to read data that was never meant to be visible to them.

Why this isn't a "set it up once" job

Network security isn't a single configuration you complete and walk away from. New devices join networks, new vulnerabilities get discovered in existing hardware and software, and network layouts change as organizations grow. A firewall rule that made sense a year ago might now be blocking something it shouldn't, or allowing something it shouldn't have. This is part of why network security is an ongoing discipline rather than a one-time project the practices above need regular review, not a single setup.

The short version

Network security protects the pathways data travels through, not just the endpoints data ends up at. It's one of the earliest topics worth learning in cyber security, because so many other types of attacks data theft, malware delivery, unauthorized access depend on reaching their target through a network in the first place. Get the network layer wrong, and it doesn't matter how well-secured the systems behind it are.

What Is a VPN?

A VPN, or Virtual Private Network, creates an encrypted connection between your device and a server operated by the VPN provider, routing your internet traffic through that server instead of sending it directly and openly. To anyone trying to observe your traffic your internet provider, a public Wi-Fi network, or someone monitoring the network you're connected to your activity appears as encrypted data going to the VPN server, not as readable traffic going to whatever website or service you're actually using.

That's the mechanism. What it's actually useful for depends on what you're trying to protect against, and a VPN isn't a single-purpose tool with one clear benefit it solves a few different problems, some more relevant to individuals than others.

What a VPN actually hides, and what it doesn't

A VPN encrypts your traffic between your device and the VPN server, which is genuinely useful on networks you don't control or trust - public Wi-Fi at a cafe or airport being the classic example, where anyone else on the same network could otherwise intercept unencrypted traffic. A VPN also masks your actual IP address from the websites and services you connect to, since they see the VPN server's IP address instead of yours. This is why VPNs are commonly used to appear as though you're browsing from a different country.

What a VPN does not do is make you anonymous online in some absolute sense. The VPN provider itself can typically see your real IP address and, depending on their logging policy, your traffic. If you log into an account - email, banking, social media that service still knows it's you, VPN or not. A VPN changes who can see your traffic and where it appears to come from; it doesn't erase your identity from every service you interact with.

Common reasons people and businesses use VPNs

  • Securing traffic on untrusted networks: Using public Wi-Fi without a VPN means your unencrypted traffic could potentially be read by others on that same network, depending on what protocols the sites you're visiting use.
  • Remote access to internal business networks: Many companies use VPNs to let employees securely connect to internal systems and resources from outside the office, as if they were plugged directly into the company network.
  • Bypassing geographic content restrictions: Since a VPN can make your traffic appear to originate from a different country, it's commonly used to access content or services restricted to specific regions.
  • Basic privacy from your internet provider: Without a VPN, your internet provider can typically see which domains you're connecting to, even if the content itself is encrypted via HTTPS. A VPN hides this destination information from your provider, routing it through the VPN server instead.

What actually matters when choosing one

Not every VPN provider deserves the same level of trust, since you're routing your traffic through their infrastructure instead of directly to its destination.

  • Logging policy: Whether the provider keeps records of your activity, and for how long. A "no-logs" claim is only as good as the provider's willingness to actually follow it third-party audits of a provider's no-logs claims are a stronger signal than the claim alone.
  • Jurisdiction: Which country's laws the provider operates under affects what data they can be legally compelled to hand over.
  • Protocol and encryption standard: Modern, well-reviewed protocols (like WireGuard or OpenVPN) are generally preferable to older or proprietary ones with less public security scrutiny.
  • Free versus paid: Free VPN services have to fund their infrastructure somehow, and some have been found selling user data or injecting ads a paid, reputable provider is generally the safer default if privacy is the actual goal.

A VPN isn't a replacement for basic security habits

A VPN protects the connection your traffic travels over. It doesn't protect you from a phishing email that gets your password, a weak password reused across accounts, or malware you download and run yourself. It's one layer of a broader security setup, not a substitute for the rest of it.

The short version

A VPN encrypts your traffic and masks your IP address by routing your connection through a third-party server, which is genuinely useful on untrusted networks and for remote access to internal systems. It's not a cloak of total anonymity, and its actual privacy value depends heavily on how much you trust the specific provider you're routing your traffic through.

The Best Cyber Security Courses for Advanced Learners

A quick honest note before this one: I'm still a student myself, working through certifications rather than years into a professional security career, so this isn't a personal review of having completed these programs. What follows is a researched comparison of the courses and certifications most commonly recommended once someone has moved past the beginner stage and has real fundamentals and hands-on practice under their belt.

If you're still working through networking basics, Linux, or your first certification, the beginner course roundup on this site is a better starting point than this one.

OSCP (Offensive Security Certified Professional)

The OSCP is widely regarded as one of the most respected hands-on certifications in offensive security, specifically for penetration testing. Unlike multiple-choice exams, it requires candidates to actually compromise a set of machines in a live exam environment within a fixed time window, then write a professional-style report on how they did it.

  • Good for: People specifically pursuing a penetration testing or offensive security career path who already have solid fundamentals and meaningful hands-on lab experience.
  • Worth knowing: This is not a certification to attempt without a serious amount of prior hands-on practice. It has a reputation for being genuinely difficult, and going in underprepared is a common and costly mistake.

CISSP (Certified Information Systems Security Professional)

CISSP, offered by (ISC) , is one of the most recognized certifications for security leadership and management roles rather than hands-on technical work. It covers a broad range of domains - risk management, security architecture, governance, and more at a conceptual and managerial level.

  • Good for: People aiming for senior, architect, or management-track security roles, or who already have several years of relevant work experience, which the certification actually requires as an eligibility criterion.
  • Worth knowing: CISSP requires proof of a minimum number of years of relevant professional experience to be fully certified (candidates without it can pass the exam and hold an "Associate of (ISC)2" status until they meet the requirement). It's not typically the next step immediately after a beginner certification.

SANS/GIAC courses

SANS Institute offers some of the most in-depth, specialized training available in the field, covering areas like incident response, digital forensics, penetration testing, and cloud security, with GIAC certifications attached to most of their courses.

  • Good for: People who want deep, specialist-level training in a specific area, often used by organizations to train working security professionals rather than as a typical first advanced step for individuals.
  • Worth knowing: SANS courses are known for being expensive relative to most other options on this list, which is part of why they're more commonly employer-funded than self-paid.

Hack The Box Pro Labs / Certified Penetration Testing Specialist (CPTS)

Hack The Box, known primarily for its hands-on hacking challenges, also offers structured advanced learning paths and certifications aimed at intermediate-to-advanced learners, including realistic, enterprise-style network environments to practice against.

  • Good for: People who want continued hands-on skill-building in a similar style to beginner-level Hack The Box content, but scaled up to more complex, multi-machine environments closer to real engagements.
  • Worth knowing: This works best as a complement to, not a replacement for, a recognized certification like OSCP if the goal is a certification that's widely recognized by employers.

Cloud security certifications (AWS, Microsoft SC-200/SC-100, Google)

As more organizations move infrastructure to the cloud, cloud-specific security certifications have become a common advanced specialization, each tied to one of the major providers.

  • Good for: People specializing specifically in cloud security, or already working with a particular cloud platform professionally and wanting to formalize that knowledge.
  • Worth knowing: These are platform-specific rather than general security certifications the right one depends entirely on which cloud provider you're already working with or targeting.

How these actually differ from each other

These aren't interchangeable "pick one" options the way some beginner courses are they represent genuinely different specializations:

  • OSCP: hands-on offensive security / penetration testing
  • CISSP: security management, governance, and leadership track
  • SANS/GIAC: deep specialist training in a specific technical domain
  • Hack The Box advanced paths: continued hands-on skill-building, often alongside a formal certification
  • Cloud security certs: specialization tied to a specific cloud platform

A note on readiness

A recurring theme across advanced certifications in this field is that they assume real fundamentals and practical experience already exist. Jumping to OSCP or CISSP without that foundation tends to lead to failed attempts and wasted exam fees, rather than an accelerated shortcut. If you're not sure whether you're ready for this tier yet, that's usually a sign it's worth spending more time on hands-on practice and an entry-level certification first not a reason to avoid advanced study altogether, just a case for sequencing it properly.
